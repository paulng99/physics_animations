HKDSE formula

$$ P = \eta\, I\, A \cos\theta $$
$\eta$: efficiency of the solar cells
$I$: intensity of sunlight at normal incidence (W m⁻²)
$A$: area of the panel (m²)
$\theta$: angle between the rays and the panel's normal

$A\cos\theta$ is the panel's area projected perpendicular to the rays — the only part of the beam it intercepts.

Physics note

Power peaks when the rays strike the panel head-on ($\theta = 0$). At noon the sun's altitude is $90° - \phi + \delta$, so a south-facing panel tilted at $\beta = \phi - \delta$ is perpendicular to the noon sun. For a fixed panel in Hong Kong a tilt of about 20° gives the best annual yield, while a steeper tilt favours winter and a flatter one favours summer. Efficiency $\eta$ is limited to 15–25% because photons below the band-gap energy cannot free electrons and excess photon energy is lost as heat.

Solar cells — HKDSE E3

Solar constant: above the atmosphere sunlight carries about 1370 W m⁻² (the power from the Sun divided by the area of a sphere of radius 1 AU). Absorption and scattering leave ≈ 1000 W m⁻² at the ground on a clear day at normal incidence.

Why $\cos\theta$? A beam of cross-section $A\cos\theta$ falls on a panel of area $A$ tilted by $\theta$ from the beam, so the power intercepted is $I A\cos\theta$. The panel converts a fraction $\eta$ of this into electrical power.

Sun path: for latitude $\phi$, declination $\delta$ and hour angle $H = 15°\times(t - 12\text{ h})$, the altitude $a$ obeys $\sin a = \sin\phi\sin\delta + \cos\phi\cos\delta\cos H$. The simulation uses this to place the Sun.

Daily energy is the area under the $P$–$t$ graph: $E = \int P\,dt$, shown in kWh (1 kWh = 3.6 MJ). Compare the tilted and flat panels to see why installers tilt panels towards the equator.
