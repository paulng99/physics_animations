HKDSE formula

$$ P = \tfrac12 \rho A v^3 $$
$\rho$: air density (kg m⁻³)
$A$: area swept by the blades, $\pi r^2$ (m²)
$v$: wind speed (m s⁻¹)

Derivation: mass of air arriving per second $= \rho A v$; its kinetic energy per second $= \tfrac12(\rho A v)v^2$.

Power against wind speed (cubic law)

Physics note

Because $P \propto v^3$, a site with twice the average wind speed offers eight times the power — this is why turbines are placed on ridges and offshore. Because $P \propto r^2$, doubling the blade length gives four times the power, which explains the trend towards ever-larger turbines. A real turbine extracts only a fraction $\eta$ of the wind's kinetic energy (Betz limit ≈ 59%), because the air must keep moving to leave the rotor.

Wind power — HKDSE E3

Consider a cylinder of air of cross-sectional area $A$ (the rotor's swept area) moving at speed $v$. In one second a length $v$ of this cylinder passes the rotor, so the mass arriving per second is $\rho A v$.

Its kinetic energy per second is $\tfrac12 (\rho A v) v^2 = \tfrac12 \rho A v^3$. This is the wind power available to the turbine.

Efficiency: the electrical output is $P_{\text{out}} = \eta \cdot \tfrac12 \rho A v^3$. The Betz limit shows $\eta$ can never exceed $16/27 \approx 59\%$; commercial turbines achieve 35–45%.

Cut-in and cut-out: real turbines do not turn below ≈ 3 m s⁻¹ and are braked above ≈ 25 m s⁻¹ to prevent damage, so the cubic law applies only in between.
